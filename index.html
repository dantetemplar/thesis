Research Context

Educational Timetabling

  • NP-hard: assign courses, rooms, instructors, and groups under hard and soft constraints
  • Long research history since the 1960s — benchmarks, competitions, many algorithmic approaches
  • Case study: Innopolis DoE, single campus, ~1,000 affected students
My work sits at the intersection of combinatorial optimization and interactive decision support. Specifically, I work on Curriculum-Based Course Timetabling — an NP-hard problem where we must assign courses, rooms, instructors, and student groups to timeslots while satisfying hard constraints and optimizing soft ones. The field has a long history with competitions and benchmarks. My case study is the Department of Education at Innopolis University — a single-campus institution with roughly one thousand affected students.

Literature Review

What the Literature Actually Uses

  • Educational timetabling papers mostly report metaheuristics (GA, tabu search, SA), IP, and graph coloring
  • Standard formulation: Curriculum-Based Course Timetabling (CB-CTT); ITC benchmarks and competitions
  • Success is usually measured by quality of initial timetable generation from scratch
  • Adoption reality: most HEIs still rely on spreadsheets (Oude Vrielink et al. 2019)

Schaerf 1999 · Ceschia et al. 2023

In the literature review, I surveyed what methods researchers actually apply to university timetabling. The dominant story in papers is metaheuristics — genetic algorithms, tabu search, simulated annealing — plus integer programming and graph coloring. The standard formulation is Curriculum-Based Course Timetabling, evaluated on ITC benchmarks. Success is usually measured by the quality of initial generation from scratch. At the same time, adoption studies show that most institutions still schedule in spreadsheets.

Literature Review

A Paradox: Universal Problem, Weak Solutions

  • Every university (and many schools) builds timetables every term — a universal operational burden
  • For such a universal problem, academic effort is surprisingly thin — the field is far from mature
  • In practice, planners still rely on spreadsheets, manual fixes, and disconnected tools
  • Usable open-source products are scarce; commercial systems are often inconvenient, incomplete, or hard to adapt locally
  • The real gap is not algorithmic fashion — it is usable, deployable tooling for everyday scheduling and maintenance

Side note: CP-SAT solvers appear more in shift scheduling than in university timetabling papers — hard university constraints would suit them, but the literature rarely goes there

The main paradox I see is not about a specific solver technology or its quality. Every university — and many schools — must build and maintain timetables every term. You would expect a mature, well-funded research area with strong tooling behind it. Instead, academic attention is surprisingly limited — far less than the scale of the problem would suggest. And in practice the situation is worse: planners work in spreadsheets, patch schedules by hand, and reconcile room bookings separately. Usable open-source systems are scarce, and commercial products are frequently inconvenient, incomplete, or difficult to adapt to local rules and workflow. The odd part is that such a common problem is still weakly solved in daily use. Also, CP-SAT solvers appearing more in shift scheduling than in university papers; So, the biggest issue is the lack of sufficient, convenient tools. That is what motivated this thesis.

Literature Review

Open Problems Identified

  • Post-publication maintenance understudied vs. initial generation (Veenstra & Vis 2016 — Minimum Perturbation Problem)
  • Lifecycle gap: planners need refinement, publication, and ongoing adaptation — not one-shot solves (Kingston 2013)
  • Adoption barriers: poor workflow integration, opaque solvers, inflexible incremental edits
  • Interactivity treated as UX add-on, not part of solution quality (Müller & Barták 2001)
From this review, I identified several open problems. First, post-publication maintenance is far less studied than initial generation. Second, planners need support across the full lifecycle: generation, refinement, publication, and ongoing adaptation — not a one-shot solve. Third, adoption barriers remain: poor workflow integration, opaque solvers, and difficulty making small incremental edits. Finally, interactivity is often treated as a non-essential UI feature rather than part of what makes a solution good. More than sixty percent of institutions revise at least ten percent of lessons after publication, yet most systems still fall back to manual editing or full regeneration.

An important part of this thesis did not start with our own assistant. A large share of the work went into studying UniTime, configuring it for Innopolis, and essentially fighting the system to make it fit our needs. UniTime, developed in Purdue University, is one of the best-known academic timetabling platforms — collaborative editing, multi-stakeholder constraints, built-in optimization. On paper, it was the most promising path forward. In practice, setup alone consumed an enormous amount of time. Day-to-day use will be very inconvenient for planners. The functionality did not cover our local rules — mixed audiences, instructors who are also students, Outlook-based booking — and extending the system proved difficult. That experience with UniTime is a direct reason why the thesis pivoted to a dedicated assistant tailored to how Innopolis schedule actually works.

Open Problem

The Problem at Innopolis University

  • Current workflow: Google Sheets, ad hoc scripts, and manual Outlook room checks — no single source of truth
  • After publication, timetables still change constantly: illness, room blackouts, curriculum updates
  • Fixes cascade: move one lecture for a room blackout → Outlook still books the old room, another collision in the new chosen slot
  • Innopolis-specific constraints: instructors who are also enrolled students; visiting lecturers with burst-like availability (cluster all teaching on one or two days); conference room blackouts
  • Volume overwhelms attention: editors miss gaps (e.g. an elective never scheduled) or room conflicts buried in the spreadsheet
  • Hours of manual reconciliation across disconnected tools — multiple times every semester
At Innopolis University, scheduling still relies on Google Sheets, ad hoc scripts, and manual Outlook room checks. Timetables are edited continuously after publication — because of teacher illness, room blackouts because of events, or late curriculum changes. One fix often triggers the next: move a lecture because a hall is blocked, and suddenly the Outlook booking still points at the old room, and the new slot you pick already has another collision. On top of generic timetabling pain, Innopolis adds constraints that spreadsheets track poorly: some instructors are also enrolled as students, so teaching and attendance cannot overlap; visiting lecturers with a primary appointment elsewhere often need burst-like schedules — all their meetings on one or two days; and large conferences can suddenly block dozens of rooms. A lot of this is also plain human error under cognitive load: one person tracks thousands of cells, and it is easy to overlook that an elective simply has no slot, or that two groups share the same room at the same time — until someone reports it after publication. This happens multiple times every semester.

Optimization

Problem Formulation (CB-CTT)

  • Input: courses, components (lec / tut / lab), groups, instructors, rooms, time grid
  • Decision: assign each meeting a day, slot, instructor, and room; phase 2 may reassign rooms
  • Hard constraints: no group / instructor / room overlaps; capacity; required meetings; component ordering; instructor banned slots
  • Soft objective: 12 separate penalty terms with normalized weights (next slides)
  • Engine: Google OR-Tools CP-SAT solver
Weekly baseline generation produces the reference weekly structure. I formulate the problem as Curriculum-Based Course Timetabling. The input is courses with their components, student groups, instructors, rooms, and a time grid. Phase one assigns day, slot, instructor, and room under the combined soft objective; phase two freezes time and instructor choices and may reassign rooms only. Hard constraints enforce feasibility — no overlaps for groups, instructors, or rooms; room capacity; all required meetings scheduled; pedagogical ordering where audiences overlap; instructor banned timeslots cells forbidden. Soft goals are encoded as twelve separate penalty terms with normalized coefficients. The model is implemented and solved with Google OR-Tools CP-SAT solver.

Optimization

Soft Objective Function

\[ \min \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left\lfloor \frac{w_i \cdot K}{D_i} \right\rceil p_i \]
\(n\)
soft penalty terms (\(n = 12\), phase 1)
\(p_i\)
penalty count; \(0 \le p_i \le D_i\)
\(D_i\)
worst-case bound for \(p_i\)
\(w_i\)
term weight (\(w_i = 1\))
\(K\)
scale constant (\(K = 10^{4}\))
\(\lfloor \cdot \rceil\)
nearest-integer rounding
Phase one combines twelve soft penalty terms into one weighted sum. We normalize each term by its worst-case bound so every criterion lands in a zero-to-one range — so different criteria are comparable instead of drowning each other out. In practice that normalization improved solver convergence a lot. Coefficients are rounded to integers because the CP-SAT solver can work only with integers. Weights are uniformly equal to 1 for now; planners rely on verification reports to see what to fix, though the weights could be tuned later if needed.

Optimization — Objective

Pedagogical coherence

1

Same-day lec + tut + lab

Lecture, tutorial & lab on one day per (course, group)

p = T − # satisfied triples

2

Adjacent lec → tut

Tutorial immediately after lecture, same day

p = P − # satisfied pairs

3

Adjacent lec → tut, same room

Back-to-back lec & tut without a room change

p = P − # same-room pairs

4

Room utilization

Prefer smallest suitable room — avoid >30% oversize when a smaller one fits

p = # oversize picks

5

Wrong component order

Avoid lab before lecture or tutorial; avoid tutorial before lecture

p = # reversed meeting pairs

The first five soft terms are about pedagogical coherence — how lecture, tutorial, and lab fit together for the same group. We favor all components on one day, tutorial immediately after lecture in the same room. Room utilization pushes the solver toward a smaller suitable room instead of an oversized hall. Wrong component order catches reversed sequences — for example lab before lecture, or tutorial before lecture; while actually it is not a problem - we could skip component on first week in semester if needed. These are the criteria that matter for teaching quality, not hard feasibility.

Optimization — Objective

Student experience

6

Meetings overload

At most 5 meetings per group per day

p = # overloaded (group, day) flags

7

Distinct subjects overload

At most 3 distinct subjects per group per day

p = # days with ≥4 subjects

8

Weekend (Sat / Sun)

No classes on weekend days

p = # weekend meetings

9

After 18:00

No meetings in slots ending after 18:00

p = # late-slot meetings

10

Group spread

Compact week — active days ≤ ⌈weekly / 5⌉ per group

p = Σ excessg

Instructor experience

11

Instructor spread

Compact teaching week per instructor

p = Σ excessi (assignment-aware)

12

Instructor preferences

Penalize discouraged weekday+slot placements; banned cells are hard-forbidden

p = Σ weighted discouraged violations (× role multiplier)

Seven terms target timetable comfort for people in the schedule. Student experience covers meetings overload and distinct subjects overload, avoiding weekends and late slots, and group spread — packing a group's meetings into fewer weekdays so other days stay completely free. Instructor spread clusters teaching into fewer weekdays. Instructor preferences penalize discouraged time slots; banned slots are hard-forbidden and never scheduled.

Optimization — Phase 2

Room Choice Refinement

Separate CP-SAT solver run after phase 1 — day, slot, and instructor fixed; only rooms are reassigned.

1

Back-to-back lec → tut room mismatch

Adjacent lecture & tutorial on the same day — unify rooms when phase 1 placed them apart

p = # diff-room pairs (same-room pairs hard-fixed)

2

Room utilization

Prefer smallest suitable room — no regression from a non-oversize phase 1 pick

p = # oversize picks

3

Consecutive class / lab room swap

Same instructor, same day, back-to-back class or lab — avoid changing rooms

p = # consecutive room mismatches

Unsafe in phase 1 — the solver would dodge these by spreading meetings to different days or slots.

Phase two is a follow-up solve. Phase one assigns day, slot, instructor, and room under the twelve soft terms; then Phase two rebuilds a smaller model for the CP-SAT solver with everything except rooms frozen and reassigns rooms only — nudging adjacent lecture–tutorial pairs into the same room, preferring smaller suitable rooms without regressing good picks, and avoiding room hops between consecutive labs. These penalties are unsafe in pass one because the solver could avoid them by moving meetings apart.

Planner Workflow

Planner Interface

React + Vite · three workspaces

  • Planner configures curriculum, inspects weekly views (group / room / instructor), applies concrete edits
  • Weekly template instantiated on calendar dates — planner edits manually; checks surface conflicts
Calendar maintenance is where the planner actually works day to day. The web interface has three workspaces: timetable, settings, checks; here is shown two of them. Settings is where courses, groups, instructors, and rooms are edited in an explicit, auditable form — the same model the solver consumes. Timetable provides weekly navigation with views by group, room, and instructor. After weekly baseline generation, the planner instantiates the template on concrete calendar dates and makes edits if needed. The planner stays in control of each decision; the checks workspace shows what needs attention.

Planner Workflow

Verification & Outlook Integration

  • Checks workspace: overlaps, capacity, missing or miscounted meetings, instructor conflicts
  • Outlook: room-calendar conflicts and meetings without a booking
The checks workspace runs issue checks on the current schedule and lists concrete problems for the planner — room, group, or student double-bookings; rooms too small; components with no slot or wrong meetings per week; instructors teaching while enrolled, or placed in banned or discouraged slots. It can also flag Outlook room-booking conflicts and meetings that still lack a reservation.

System Design

System Architecture

  • Baseline generation: config → CP-SAT solver worker → weekly schedule artifact
  • Maintenance loop: planner edits in UI → verification → Outlook sync
  • FastAPI backend orchestrates solves, tasks, and WebSocket logs between React frontend and Python workers
This swimlane diagram shows how weekly baseline generation and calendar maintenance connect. The React frontend covers settings, timetable, and checks. The FastAPI backend stores schedule configuration, dispatches optimization tasks to worker nodes, and streams progress back to the UI. Python workers run the CP-SAT solver for baseline generation and verification plus Outlook sync for maintenance. The separation keeps the UI responsive while solver jobs run asynchronously. The weekly baseline flows into the timetable workspace; planner edits during maintenance trigger verification and Outlook sync.

Results

Research Contribution

Literature and practice split timetable generation from maintenance; strong solvers and usable tooling rarely meet in one system.

  • Model & optimization — CP-SAT solver on CB-CTT for a single-campus university: hard constraints, 12 normalized soft terms, feasible weekly baselines on full Innopolis data
  • Product — human-in-the-loop assistant for baseline generation and post-publication maintenance; planner edits, checks workspace, Outlook room sync; deployed at the Department of Education (~1,000 students), replacing Sheets and manual cross-checks

Contribution: capable optimization and operable software, designed and validated together

I tie the contribution back to the open problems from the literature review. On optimization, the thesis delivers a CP-SAT solver workflow on a formal CB-CTT model that scales to real institutional input — not a competition instance. On the product side, the assistant addresses the lifecycle gap Kingston describes: baseline generation and calendar maintenance in one tool, with issues flagged in verification reports. It is deployed at Innopolis DoE with Outlook integration — replacing Sheets and manual cross-checks. Both halves matter: without the solver, the checks are hollow; without the product, the solver does not reach daily operations.

Results

Early Exploration: Spreadsheet Parser & Plugin

  • Parser converts heterogeneous Google Sheets schedules into structured CB-CTT config for the optimizer
  • Plugin prototype adds in-sheet conflict detection — promising for validating the approach
  • Limit: spreadsheets are too vague and unrestricted to build a reliable, rule-enforced system on top
  • Both informed the pivot to a dedicated web assistant
Before the full assistant, I explored staying inside the planner's existing toolchain. I built a spreadsheet parser that reads heterogeneous Google Sheets and converts them into structured CB-CTT configuration the optimizer expects — bridging the gap between ad hoc sheets and solver input. I also prototyped a Google Sheets plugin that flags conflicts such as room over-capacity or instructor double-booking. This confirmed that automated checks are valuable and that planners would use them. But spreadsheets are inherently vague — implicit conventions, free-form cells. A proper system needs reproducible solves and integrated checks. That is why the thesis pivoted from plugin to dedicated assistant, while keeping the parser as a migration path from legacy sheets.

Summary

Limitations & Future Work

Limitations

  • Validated on a small university (~1,000 students) — scale not yet proven
  • Limited data on instructor time preferences in evaluation
  • UX and rollout are functional but not polished; adoption path needs work
  • Too tailored to Innopolis rules — not yet generic for all HEIs and schools

Future work

  • AI assist: natural-language requests (“room 105 blocked — move the lecture”) with solver-backed suggestions; text → structured preferences
  • Finer-grained optimizer tuning
  • Plugin system for institutional extensions (booking, SIS, custom checks)
  • Code quality, documentation, and path toward a widely usable open-source product

Questions?

To conclude: the system has clear limitations. It was validated at a relatively small university, with limited data on instructor preferences. The user experience and deployment story work in practice, but they are not fully polished yet. And the current model is still closely tied to Innopolis-specific rules — the long-term goal is something that fits universities and schools more broadly, not one institution. Future work can address these gaps and go further. One direction is AI assistance: a planner could say in plain language that room 105 will be occupied and a lecture must move, and the system would propose feasible alternatives; or describe instructor preferences in text and have them converted into structured config. Another direction is finer optimizer tuning. A plugin architecture would make it easier to add booking systems, SIS hooks, or custom checks without forking the core. Finally, improving code quality and documentation could turn this from a thesis prototype into a credible open-source scheduling tool. Thank you for your attention. I am happy to take your questions.
